3. PROJECT OBJECTIVES  (what does it solve?)
================================================================

Razorpay has launched Agentic Payments on UPI Reserve Pay, so an AI agent can
now spend a person's money on their behalf. The human approves a spending
limit up front, and the agent goes shopping on its own.

The problem is what that limit actually covers. UPI can confirm two things:
the money is there, and the merchant is the right one. It cannot confirm the
third and most important one - is this the thing the human actually asked for?

A concrete example. You tell your agent "buy running shoes under Rs 5,000". It
comes back with running shoes at Rs 4,200, plus an extended protection plan
that the merchant's own product page talked it into adding. Right merchant.
Under the limit. Valid signature. Every check the rail can perform passes. But
you never asked for the protection plan, and when you dispute the charge the
merchant cannot prove what you did authorise, because "the agent decided to"
is not a defence anybody has had to answer before. At agent speed - a hundred
plausible carts a minute - that becomes a chargeback problem nobody is
currently positioned to contest.

Parchi is the missing check, and it sits exactly where Razorpay already sits:
between the agent and the merchant.

Every agent purchase must carry a signed permission slip - an AP2-inspired
intent mandate holding the human's spending cap, the allowed categories, the
allowed payment methods, an expiry, a one-time nonce, and the agent's own
playback of what it understood the human to ask for.

Twelve deterministic checks run first: signature, expiry, payee, method, line
items, quantity, prices, category, discount, cap, agent identity, replay.
Plain code, no AI in that file, stopping at the first failure. Only a cart
that has already passed all twelve is worth spending a model call on, and that
call answers exactly one question rules cannot: does this cart match what the
human actually asked for?

There are three verdicts, not two - allow, block, and ask the human. A system
with only the first two is a filter; the third is what makes it a risk
product. If the model call is slow or dies, the answer degrades to "ask",
never to "allow". Every decision, whichever way it goes, is written to a
hash-chained ledger with a dispute evidence pack, so the merchant can prove
afterwards what was authorised.

I scored it like a risk product rather than like a demo: 1,000 labelled agent
purchases, with false positives counted in rupees rather than percentages,
because a wrongly blocked customer is real revenue the merchant lost and a
percentage hides that.

  Block all agent traffic   caught everything, refused Rs 33,73,331 of good revenue
  Allow everything          paid out Rs 17,83,157 on violations
  Rules only                235 of 280 caught, Rs 2,19,908 leaked
  Parchi                    278 of 280 caught, 22 false blocks, Rs 1,59,521 total

Two honest notes on that table. The Parchi row is a run against a real model,
not an offline stand-in, because a row produced by a stand-in is not evidence
for what this project claims. And a perfect score on data I generated, against
attacks I designed, defended by rules I wrote, would be a closed loop - so I
had a separate model, shown the product and none of my rules, checks or
thresholds, write the attacks instead. Against those, Parchi catches 76%, and
the seven that got through are named individually in the README rather than
summarised away.

There is also an operations console for the staff side, because a risk product
is a verdict plus the person who has to answer for it. Every consequential
action is attributed by name in the ledger, and the AI is never the last actor
on anything that costs a customer money: it can cool an account for ten
minutes and it can propose a refund, but a person releases the one and
approves the other.

Strictly defence-only: nothing in this repository can initiate a payment.
Every path is a verifier or a blocker - by construction, not by assertion.

6. BUILD CHALLENGES & TECHNICAL OBSTACLES
================================================================

Three worth naming, all measured rather than remembered. The full log runs to
22 entries.

1. The AI I built to block attackers was blocking innocent customers instead.

Parchi has a second layer that watches behaviour across many purchases rather
than inside a single cart, and an AI adjudicator that reads those patterns and
can lock an account for ten minutes. I tested it against every attack I could
think of, it caught all of them, and it looked finished.

Then something bothered me. Every deterministic check in the repo is scored
against a thousand labelled rows. The one component in the whole system that
can refuse a real, paying customer had been scored against nothing at all. I
had only ever tested it on attackers.

So I wrote the evaluation that should have existed first: twelve situations,
half of them ordinary customers who happen to trip a counter - an office
manager buying for a team, somebody retrying on bad wifi, a family sharing one
account. It convicted fifteen of eighteen benign cases.

The lesson was that its perfect recall had never been evidence of anything. A
rule that always says "yes, attack" also catches every attack. My prompt
listed what attacks look like and never once said what clearing an innocent
person looks like, so the model had no reason to clear anybody.

The fix was to write the cost asymmetry into the prompt itself: convicting a
real customer blocks someone mid-purchase who did nothing wrong, whereas
clearing a real attacker costs very little, because every deterministic
refusal still stands underneath. That took it to 18 of 18 attacks caught with
16 of 18 ordinary customers left alone, and eval/adjudicator.py re-runs the
measurement rather than asking anyone to take my word for it.

2. The evidence published beside my headline numbers was from a different run.

I had a results file and a ledger file sitting next to each other as proof of
my best model run. Both were internally valid. Then I checked the timestamps
and found the ledger had finished 5,550 seconds before the run it was supposed
to be evidence for had even started, and the results file pointed at a third
file that a later run had since overwritten.

The cause was mundane: every full batch wrote to the same ledger path, so
"publishing" a run quietly depended on my remembering to copy a file at the
right moment. Nothing crashed, nothing looked wrong, and every artefact
verified on its own. Only the claim that one was evidence for the other was
false.

The fix was to make the coupling explicit - evaluate.py now takes an explicit
--ledger path - and to add a test that asserts a published ledger's records
actually fall inside the time window of the run reporting them, so this cannot
happen silently again. I then re-ran the batch on a clean tree.

The honest consequence is that the numbers got worse. Recall moved from 97.1%
to 99.3%, but false blocks moved from 12 to 22, and I published the
worse-looking half because it is the half I can hand someone the evidence for.

3. Two prompt fixes that were obviously right measured worse than doing
nothing.

The intent-matching model was occasionally re-deciding the spending cap, which
is arithmetic the deterministic checks already own. So I told it in the prompt
to leave the cap alone. It stopped doing that and started inventing a
different price judgement instead. So I forbade price reasoning entirely, and
that was worse again.

Three full batches and about two hours to arrive back where I started. The
generalisable lesson, and the reason it is written down: adding a rule to a
prompt does not delete a behaviour, it moves it. Both measured tables are in
the failure log so the claim can be checked rather than believed.

Full log, 22 entries, each with what I assumed, what it actually turned out to
be, and what it cost:
https://github.com/DarkBird10020/parchi/blob/main/FAILURES.md
